For the people with budget

a compiler.

You didn't buy Kubernetes because you wanted it. You bought it because the alternative was worse. The bill arrives on day-2 — quietly, as a drag on every team — and it's the bill nobody is itemizing.

The bottleneck that isn't on the org chart

Every cluster has a Luca.

Luca is the one engineer who reviews every infrastructure change. Not because there's a policy that says he has to. Because nobody else can safely assess the blast radius.

Luca holds the mental model. He knows which Service selectors are load-bearing. He knows which two charts share a port by coincidence. He knows which ConfigMap is read by three other services that nobody annotated. He is, functionally, the compiler.

When Luca is on vacation, deployments queue. When Luca is overloaded, developers wait — two days, five days, a week. When Luca leaves, six months of tribal knowledge walks out the door and the cluster becomes a museum: too fragile to change without him.

The problem isn't that you need Luca. The problem is that every change needs Luca.

Where the money actually goes

Day-2 is the invisible line on the infrastructure bill.

Luca's salary is the smallest part of the cost. The real cost is the queue behind him — and the incidents that happened while he was doing someone else's job.

Developer time in the review queue
Every change routed through one person, because nobody else can prove it's safe. Teams miss their delivery windows. Features ship a sprint late because the infrastructure change was a sprint late. Low end: $150k/yr. High end: $400k/yr per org.
Config-change incidents you attributed to "the deploy"
A selector mismatch nobody reviewed. A port that used to be 8080 and isn't anymore. A NetworkPolicy that nobody updated when the new service launched. The post-mortem calls it a process issue. It's a compiler-shaped hole.
The audit season
Three weeks, every quarter. Screenshots of Argo dashboards. kubectl output dumps. Helm release histories reconstructed into spreadsheets. Engineers who should be shipping product producing evidence about the product. None of the evidence is cryptographically verifiable. All of it is fakeable.
The onboarding tax
A new engineer takes weeks to be trusted with a real change — not because they're slow, because there's no compiler to catch their mistakes. The invariants Luca enforces live in Luca's head. Until they don't.

None of these show up as a line on an invoice. They show up as velocity. You notice the cost in hindsight, when a competitor who doesn't have it ships faster.

The forcing function already happened

Day-2 cost just moved from invisible to a line on the budget.

For a decade, the cost of bad day-2 was diffuse enough to ignore. Everyone had it. Nobody paid it in one number. Then Broadcom acquired VMware, raised prices, and turned the cost of bad day-2 into a single renewal line item.

  • A UK university renewal went from £40k to £500k.
  • AT&T faced a reported 1,050% increase.
  • A mid-market bank we talked to saw its VMware spend double in one cycle and decided it was cheaper to migrate.

The people leaving VMware aren't optimists. They lived through day-2 on a stack they thought was stable, and it cost them. They are arriving on Kubernetes knowing exactly what day-2 pain looks like — and looking for a way to not buy into it twice.

This is the most receptive buyer on the market. They understand the problem viscerally. They're buying a new stack from scratch. They can purchase day-2 safety upfront, before they accumulate the debt that would otherwise make migration impossible in five years.

The number nobody else can claim

One engineer, 10–15 clusters. Not 3–5.

In the incumbent stack, operating Kubernetes at scale is a labor problem. Each additional cluster adds O(n) wiring to maintain. Industry benchmarks land at 3–5 clusters per SRE.

On a compiler-backed stack, each additional cluster adds O(log n) — because the compiler handles the cross-cluster concerns (drift detection, policy propagation, migration orchestration) that you currently pay humans to handle. Our target ratio is 10–15 clusters per engineer.

To be clear: that's the ratio we're aiming for, grounded in what nixpkgs achieves at the OS layer. We don't have production customers yet. If you'd like to be the benchmark that validates it, read on.

One innovation, many consequences

Day-2-as-compile-time is the foundation. Everything sits on top.

You don't buy "a compiler for Kubernetes." You buy a specific consequence. The compiler is the reason the consequence is possible.

Managed operations

10:1 margin on a commodity service.

Running Kubernetes for others is a labor business — unless your operators manage 10x the fleet per head. That ratio is a consequence of the compiler, not the team.

Compliance evidence

DORA / NIS2 / SOC2 as a build artifact.

The evidence is a cryptographically verifiable graph, not a screenshot. Auditors read a file in the store. Your engineers don't spend three weeks a quarter assembling spreadsheets.

Internal developer platform

The catalog can't lie.

Backstage / Port / your homegrown IDP are accurate on the day you set them up and drift from day two. The kix catalog is derived from the deployment — same source, same hash, provably consistent.

AI-assisted operations

Let the agent write. Let the compiler prove.

Agents generate manifests well. They can't reason about cross-service impact across your whole cluster. A compiler can. If you're letting AI touch your cluster, you want something between it and production that checks the blast radius.

VMware migration

The people leaving VMware aren't shopping for Kubernetes tools. They're shopping for the stack they won't have to leave again in 2034. Day-2 safety from day one is that stack.

The question you're actually asking

Will this still exist in three years?

Fair question. Here's what we can say honestly:

  • The code is open. kix is a Nix flake. If we disappeared tomorrow, everything you deployed keeps running, and you can fork the repo. Your cluster's manifests are standard Kubernetes YAML — nothing proprietary between you and kubectl.
  • The design is public. Every open question is in v2_discussions/. Every shipped decision is in v2_discussions/archive/. No hidden roadmap, no private architecture deck.
  • The foundation is shipped. The parts of this argument you've been reading — the compiler, the DAG, the CLI, deploy/diff/rollback, SBOM/SLSA exports — run today. The apex (stateful migration, operator swap) is on the roadmap. We say so on every page.
  • We're looking for design partners, not charging you. If you become a partner, you shape the roadmap. If this turns out not to be for you, you owe us nothing and you leave with standard Kubernetes YAML. Low cost of trying, low cost of leaving.
